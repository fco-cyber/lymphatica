    For families

    Three things worth knowing early

    1. Many lymphomas can be cured.

      In the United States, 5-year relative survival for Hodgkin lymphoma is 89.3% for people diagnosed in 2016–2022. Relative survival compares people with lymphoma with people of the same age who don't have it. About 6 in 10 people with diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (the most common fast-growing type, which starts in B cells, the white blood cells that make antibodies) are cured by standard first treatment, and newer treatments now cure some people whose lymphoma comes back.

    2. Slow-growing lymphomas are often watched before they are treated.

      When a slow-growing (indolent) lymphoma isn't causing problems, starting treatment straight away doesn't help people live longer. In a UK trial, about 1 in 5 people who were carefully monitored still hadn't needed chemotherapy 10 years later.

    3. Treatment keeps improving.

      Since 2022, large trials have found better results with new combinations for Hodgkin lymphoma, large B-cell lymphoma, follicular lymphoma and mantle cell lymphoma, often with fewer side effects. See what's new.

    Survival figures describe large groups of people diagnosed years ago. They can't predict what will happen to one person, and they don't yet reflect the newest treatments.

    Understand

    What lymphoma is

    Lymphoma is a cancer of lymphocytes, the white blood cells that protect you from infection. It usually shows up in lymph nodes, but it can start almost anywhere in the body.

    Your lymphatic system

    The lymphatic system is a network of fine vessels and small, bean-shaped glands called lymph nodes. The vessels carry lymph, a clear fluid (the word comes from the Latin for water), from the tissues back towards the bloodstream. Lymph nodes sit along the vessels like beads on a thread. They act as checkpoints where lymphocytes meet germs and abnormal cells.

    Lymph nodes cluster in the neck, armpits, chest, abdomen and groin. The spleen (an organ under the left ribs that filters blood), the bone marrow (the soft centre of bones, where blood cells are made) and the thymus (a gland behind the breastbone) are part of the same system.

    What goes wrong

    There are two main kinds of lymphocyte. B cells make antibodies, the proteins that tag germs for destruction. T cells attack infected cells and direct the immune response. Lymphoma starts when one lymphocyte picks up changes in its genes and keeps multiplying when it should stop. Most lymphomas start in B cells; fewer start in T cells.

    Lymphocytes normally travel around the body, so lymphoma is often found in several places when it is diagnosed. That is part of how this cancer behaves, and many people whose lymphoma is widespread are still cured.

    Two families, and two speeds

    Hodgkin lymphoma is recognised under the microscope by a small number of large abnormal cells, called Reed–Sternberg cells, surrounded by many normal immune cells. It is most common in teenagers and young adults, but it can affect people of any age. In the United States, the median age at diagnosis is 38.

    Non-Hodgkin lymphoma covers all the other types, of which there are dozens. It makes up about 90 in every 100 lymphomas diagnosed in Australia.

    Doctors also describe lymphomas by speed. Aggressive (fast-growing) types grow over weeks to months. They usually need treatment soon, and many are curable. Indolent (slow-growing) types grow over months to years. Many people live with them for a long time, sometimes without needing treatment at first. Occasionally a slow-growing lymphoma changes into a fast-growing one, which doctors call .

    Symptoms that can point to lymphoma

    • A painless swelling in the neck, armpit or groin that lasts more than 2 weeks, or doesn't settle after an infection is treated
    • Fevers without a clear cause
    • Drenching night sweats
    • Losing weight without trying
    • Persistent, severe itching
    • Frequent infections
    • Rarely, pain in swollen lymph nodes after drinking alcohol

    Fevers, drenching sweats and losing more than a tenth of your body weight over six months are called B symptoms. Doctors record them because they help describe the lymphoma.

    Most swollen glands, fevers and night sweats are caused by infections or other common problems, not lymphoma. If symptoms last, see your GP (family doctor).

    What causes it?

    For most people, no cause is ever found. Lymphoma is not contagious, and it is not caused by anything you did or didn't do.

    Large studies pooling thousands of people have linked some types with autoimmune diseases, certain infections such as hepatitis C, and a family history of lymphoma. Lymphoma is also more common when immunity is weakened, for example by medicines taken after an organ transplant. A stomach germ called Helicobacter pylori is linked with gastric MALT lymphoma, a slow-growing stomach lymphoma that can respond to antibiotics. Most people with these risk factors never develop lymphoma.

    Non-Hodgkin lymphoma is the sixth most commonly diagnosed cancer in Australia.     For GPs, physiotherapists, osteopaths and other cliniciansRed flags, referral timeframes and hands-on care

    Refer when you see lymph node swelling lasting more than 2 weeks, nodes with systemic symptoms, nodes that don't settle after an infection is treated, node pain after alcohol, any unexplained lump or mass, or unexplained fever, drenching sweats, weight loss, persistent severe itch or frequent infections.

    Timeframes in Australia's Optimal Care Pathway: specialist referral within 72 hours if lymphoma is highly likely, within 4 weeks if concerning features are absent, and a GP review within 6 weeks if you are watching a node.

    Musculoskeletal presentations. Lymphoma occasionally presents as bone pain, especially at night, or back pain with systemic symptoms. New leg weakness or numbness, a band of altered sensation around the trunk, or new bladder or bowel changes suggest spinal cord compression and need same-day emergency assessment.

    Hands-on care during and after treatment. Avoid firm pressure over enlarged nodes, known lymphoma sites, central lines (PICC lines and ports, thin tubes placed into a large vein for treatment) and skin being treated with radiotherapy. Take care when platelets (the blood cells that help clotting) are low, and with bones weakened by long courses of steroids. Postpone treatment during fever or infection. These are consensus precautions, not trial findings.

    Understand

    Types of lymphoma

    The type is the name a pathologist (a doctor who diagnoses disease by examining tissue) gives the lymphoma. It shapes almost everything: how fast it grows, which treatment is used and what to expect. Choose a type to read more.

    This list covers the more common types. The two current classifications, the WHO 5th edition and the International Consensus Classification (both 2022), describe many more.

    Understand

    Tests, staging and scores

    A diagnosis of lymphoma always rests on a biopsy. Other tests then show where the lymphoma is and help your team plan treatment.

    The biopsy

    A biopsy is a sample of tissue examined under the microscope. For lymphoma, doctors usually need a whole lymph node (an excision biopsy) or several thin cores of tissue taken with a needle (a core biopsy), because the pattern of cells matters as well as the cells themselves. A tissue diagnosis is needed before definitive treatment starts.

    A haematopathologist (a doctor who specialises in diagnosing blood cancers from tissue) then uses special stains and genetic tests to name the exact type. For some fast-growing B-cell lymphomas, finding rearranged genes (genes that have broken and joined another chromosome), such as MYC and BCL2, changes the name and the treatment plan.

    PET-CT scan

    A PET-CT combines a CT scan (detailed X-ray pictures) with a PET scan, which shows how actively cells take up a slightly radioactive sugar called FDG. Most lymphomas take up more sugar than normal tissue, so they show as bright areas. PET-CT is the standard scan for staging most lymphomas, and staging should be completed within 2 weeks.

    Your scan team will give you instructions. These often include fasting for a few hours and avoiding hard exercise the day before, because working muscles also take up the sugar.

    Because PET-CT shows the bone marrow, a bone marrow biopsy is no longer needed routinely for Hodgkin lymphoma and most diffuse large B-cell lymphoma. Other types may still need one.

    Blood tests and checks before treatment

    • Full blood count, and kidney and liver tests.
    • LDH (lactate dehydrogenase), an enzyme released when cells turn over quickly. A high level can signal more active lymphoma and is part of some risk scores.
    • Hepatitis B, hepatitis C and HIV tests. Everyone starting treatment should be tested for hepatitis B. Some treatments, especially anti-CD20 antibodies (medicines that latch onto a marker called CD20 on B cells) such as rituximab, can reawaken a hidden hepatitis B infection; antiviral tablets prevent this.
    • A heart check, usually an echocardiogram (an ultrasound of the heart), before anthracycline chemotherapy such as doxorubicin, which can strain the heart.
    • A fertility conversation before treatment if you might want children in future.
    • Sometimes a lumbar puncture (a sample of spinal fluid) or a brain scan, when there is a risk of spread to the brain.

    Staging: where the lymphoma is

    Lymphoma is staged from I to IV using the Lugano system. The landmark is the diaphragm, the dome-shaped breathing muscle under the lungs. Choose a stage to see an example.

    Treatment is usually planned around two broad groups: limited (stages I and II without a very large mass) and advanced (stages III and IV). A very large mass is called bulky disease. Doctors may add the letter E when lymphoma has grown from a node into a nearby organ, and, for Hodgkin lymphoma only, A or B to show whether B symptoms are present.

    Advanced stage is common in lymphoma and does not mean "incurable". In one trial of people with stage III or IV Hodgkin lymphoma, 92% were alive without their lymphoma progressing 2 years after nivolumab-AVD treatment.

    Reading PET results: the Deauville score

    PET scans during and after treatment are scored from 1 to 5 on the Deauville scale, which compares how bright any remaining area is with normal tissue in the chest and liver. Scores of 1 to 3 usually mean no active lymphoma can be seen, called a complete metabolic response. Scores of 4 and 5 mean some activity remains. That can be lymphoma, but it can also be inflammation or healing, so your team reads it alongside everything else.

    Risk scores

    Risk scores combine a few facts to estimate how a group of people with similar features tends to do. The International Prognostic Index (IPI), used for fast-growing non-Hodgkin lymphoma, counts five factors: age over 60, advanced stage, a raised LDH, reduced day-to-day function, and lymphoma in more than one site outside the lymph nodes. Other types have their own scores, such as FLIPI for follicular lymphoma. Scores guide treatment choices and trial entry. They describe groups and cannot tell you how your own treatment will go.

    Scans after treatment

    A PET-CT at the end of treatment shows how well it worked. After that, if you are well, routine scans are generally not recommended. In a large US study of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, most relapses (the lymphoma coming back) were found because of symptoms. Scheduled scans found relapse before symptoms in only 1.6% of people, and survival after relapse was no better when it was found at a scheduled visit than between visits.

    Waiting for scans and results is hard. Mind and emotions has ways to manage "scanxiety".

    Treatment

    What to expect, step by step

    Most people move through the same stages. The timeframes come from Australia's Optimal Care Pathway for Hodgkin and diffuse large B-cell lymphomas, the national standard for good care for these two common types. If you live elsewhere, your country may have different targets, so ask your team what to expect.

    1. Something doesn't feel rightGP (family doctor) visit, first tests and referral

      Your GP examines you and may arrange blood tests, imaging and a biopsy. If lymphoma seems highly likely, you should be referred to a specialist within 72 hours; otherwise within 4 weeks. If your GP is watching a swollen gland, a review within 6 weeks is recommended.

      What helps: write down your symptoms and when they started, and ask who will contact you with results.

    2. DiagnosisThe biopsy names the type

      Results usually come from a haematologist (a doctor who specialises in blood cancers). Hearing the word "lymphoma" can make it hard to take anything else in. That is normal.

      • Bring someone with you, or ask whether you can record the conversation.
      • Ask for the exact name of the type to be written down.
      • Lymphoma nurses can explain things again later. In Australia, Lymphoma Australia's nurse line is 1800 953 081; Support services lists lines in other countries.
    3. Staging and planningPET-CT, a team meeting, and checks before treatment

      Staging tests should be finished within 2 weeks. A multidisciplinary team (haematologists, pathologists, radiologists, radiation oncologists and nurses who plan care together) should discuss your case before treatment starts. In urgent situations treatment may begin first, with the plan confirmed afterwards.

      Before treatment, ask about:

      • Fertility preservation, such as sperm banking or egg or embryo freezing.
      • Vaccines, ideally before treatment, and vaccines for the people you live with.
      • Hepatitis B testing.
      • A heart check if you are having anthracycline chemotherapy.
      • Whether you will need a PICC line or port (a thin tube placed into a large vein so treatment and blood tests don't need repeated needles).
      • Work, money and carers. Hospital social workers can help.
    4. Starting treatmentUsually within 2 weeks of diagnosis and staging

      Treatment should start within 2 weeks of diagnosis and staging, or within 24 hours if vital organs are under pressure or the lymphoma is growing fast.

      The first day is often the longest. Antibody drips are given slowly at first because some people react during the first dose with chills, a rash or breathlessness; nurses watch closely and can slow or pause the drip. For fast-growing lymphoma, you may get extra fluids and medicine to prevent tumour lysis syndrome, a chemical imbalance that can happen when many lymphoma cells break down at once.

    5. During treatmentCycles, check-ups and looking after yourself

      Most chemotherapy is given in cycles: a treatment day followed by a rest period, often 2 to 4 weeks long, so the body can recover. You will have blood tests before each cycle. Some types have a PET scan partway through to check the response.

      • Know your fever plan: a temperature of 38°C (100.4°F) or more needs an urgent call.
      • Keep moving within your energy. Exercise reduces fatigue during treatment.
      • Eat what you can, and ask for a dietitian if eating is hard.
      • Tell your team about low mood or worry; it is common and treatable.
    6. Finishing treatmentEnd-of-treatment scan and your written plan

      A PET-CT usually checks the result. You, a carer if you wish, and your GP should receive a treatment summary and follow-up plan, including which tests you need and when, the late effects to watch for, and how to get back in quickly if you are worried.

      Many people expect to feel relieved and are surprised to feel flat, tired or anxious instead. Losing the routine and the close contact with the team is a big change. This is common and usually eases with time and support.

    7. Recovery and follow-upRegular reviews, usually without routine scans

      Follow-up visits check how you are feeling, examine you and review blood tests. If you are well, routine scans usually aren't needed. Your energy, immunity and fitness rebuild over months. Recovery and life after treatment has a personal late-effects checklist.

    8. If lymphoma comes backThere are usually further options

      Relapse is usually found through symptoms or tests between visits. Options depend on the type and your health. At first relapse of Hodgkin lymphoma or diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, they include further chemotherapy with a stem cell transplant using your own cells, CAR T-cell therapy (your own immune cells reprogrammed to attack lymphoma) and bispecific antibodies (medicines that link your immune cells to lymphoma cells). Clinical trials are worth asking about at any relapse.

    9. Palliative care, at any stageSupport for symptoms and quality of life

      Palliative care is specialist care for symptoms, comfort and quality of life. It can be offered early, alongside treatment aimed at the lymphoma, based on need rather than prognosis, and it also supports families.

    Treatment

    Treatments explained

    Most people have a combination of treatments. Your haematologist will explain why a particular plan suits your type, stage, health and wishes.

    Active monitoring ("watch and wait")For slow-growing lymphoma that isn't causing problems
    What it is
    Regular check-ups and blood tests instead of immediate treatment, with treatment started if symptoms appear or the lymphoma grows.
    Why it's used
    For symptom-free, slow-growing lymphoma, early treatment doesn't help people live longer, and some people never need treatment.
    What it's like
    Many people find waiting harder than they expected. It helps to know which symptoms to report and how quickly you can be seen.
    Chemotherapy and chemo-immunotherapyFor example R-CHOP, ABVD and bendamustine-rituximab
    What it is
    Medicines that kill rapidly dividing cells, usually combined. Chemo-immunotherapy adds an antibody such as rituximab. R-CHOP is rituximab with cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine and prednisone; ABVD is doxorubicin, bleomycin, vinblastine and dacarbazine.
    What it's like
    Given by drip, injection or tablets in cycles, usually every 2 to 4 weeks for about 3 to 6 months, mostly as a day patient. G-CSF injections (a growth factor that speeds the recovery of infection-fighting white cells) are often given between doses.
    Common effects
    Low blood counts and infection risk, tiredness, nausea, hair loss, mouth soreness, constipation, tingling in hands and feet (especially with vincristine) and effects on fertility.
    Evidence
    Some people need less than once thought: young people with small, early-stage aggressive B-cell lymphoma did as well with four cycles of R-CHOP as with six, with fewer side effects.
    Monoclonal antibodiesRituximab, obinutuzumab
    What it is
    Laboratory-made antibodies that lock onto CD20, a marker on B cells, and flag them for the immune system to destroy.
    What it's like
    A drip or an injection under the skin. Reactions such as chills or a rash are most likely with the first dose.
    Points to know
    Normal B cells are cleared too, so antibody levels and vaccine responses can stay low for months, and re-vaccination may be needed later. Hepatitis B testing beforehand is essential.
    Antibody–drug conjugatesBrentuximab vedotin, polatuzumab vedotin
    What it is
    An antibody joined to a chemotherapy drug, which carries the drug straight to lymphoma cells. Brentuximab vedotin targets CD30 (on Hodgkin and some T-cell lymphomas); polatuzumab vedotin targets CD79b (on B cells).
    Common effects
    Tingling or numbness in hands and feet. In a large Hodgkin lymphoma trial, this was more common with brentuximab vedotin, but it improved or went away in most people.
    Evidence
    Brentuximab vedotin with AVD chemotherapy helped people with advanced Hodgkin lymphoma live longer than ABVD. Pola-R-CHP kept more people with higher-risk large B-cell lymphoma free of progression than R-CHOP.
    Immune checkpoint inhibitorsNivolumab, pembrolizumab
    What it is
    Medicines that release a "brake" (PD-1) on T cells so they can attack lymphoma. Hodgkin lymphoma is especially sensitive to them.
    Common effects
    Usually well tolerated. Less often the immune system inflames healthy organs such as the thyroid, skin, bowel, liver or lungs, sometimes months later. Report new symptoms promptly.
    Evidence
    Nivolumab-AVD kept more people with stage III–IV Hodgkin lymphoma progression-free than brentuximab vedotin-AVD, with fewer people stopping treatment early. It is now a standard first treatment, approved in the US in March 2026.
    Targeted tabletsBTK inhibitors, BCL2 inhibitors, lenalidomide
    What it is
    Tablets that block signals lymphoma cells depend on. BTK inhibitors (ibrutinib, acalabrutinib, zanubrutinib, pirtobrutinib) block a growth signal in B cells. BCL2 inhibitors (venetoclax, sonrotoclax) switch off a survival protein. Lenalidomide boosts immune attack and is often paired with rituximab (called R²).
    Common effects
    Low blood counts, bruising or bleeding, high blood pressure and an irregular heartbeat (atrial fibrillation) with BTK inhibitors, and tumour lysis risk when venetoclax starts, so the dose is built up slowly with blood tests.
    Food and medicines
    Some of these tablets interact with foods and other medicines. For example, people taking ibrutinib are advised to avoid grapefruit and Seville oranges. Check every supplement and new medicine with your pharmacist.
    RadiotherapyFocused X-ray treatment to involved areas
    What it is
    High-energy X-rays aimed at the area involved, usually given on weekdays over 2 to 4 weeks. It is used for limited or bulky disease, often after chemotherapy, and sometimes on its own.
    What it's like
    Each session takes minutes and doesn't hurt. Effects depend on the area: tiredness, skin redness, a sore throat or dry mouth.
    Evidence
    A PET scan after chemotherapy can show who can safely skip radiotherapy. In early-stage Hodgkin lymphoma, giving radiotherapy only to people whose PET still showed activity worked almost as well as giving it to everyone (5-year progression-free survival 95.1% vs 97.3%), sparing many people its long-term risks.
    Stem cell transplantHigh-dose chemotherapy with stem cell rescue
    Autologous
    Your own stem cells (the cells that make all blood cells) are collected and frozen. You then have high-dose chemotherapy, and the cells are returned to rebuild your bone marrow. It cures many people whose Hodgkin lymphoma comes back, and is used at relapse in other types.
    Allogeneic
    Stem cells come from a donor, adding a new immune system that can fight the lymphoma. It is used for selected people because the risks are higher.
    What it's like
    Several weeks in or near hospital, then months of recovery. Childhood vaccines need to be given again afterwards.
    CAR T-cell therapyYour own immune cells, reprogrammed
    What it is
    Your T cells are collected through a drip in a process like donating blood (apheresis), changed in a laboratory to recognise a lymphoma marker (usually CD19), grown, and given back as a single infusion after a few days of mild chemotherapy. Making the cells takes a few weeks; some people have "bridging" treatment meanwhile.
    Side effects
    Cytokine release syndrome (CRS): fever, low blood pressure or low oxygen as the immune system activates. ICANS (immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome): confusion, word-finding or handwriting difficulty, drowsiness and, rarely, seizures. Both are usually treatable when caught early. Blood counts can stay low for weeks to months, raising infection risk.
    Staying close
    You need a carer and must stay near the treatment centre after infusion, and not drive for a time. In June 2025 the US shortened both periods to 2 weeks. Rules differ between countries and centres, so follow your own team's instructions.
    Evidence
    For large B-cell lymphoma that comes back within a year, CAR T-cell therapy helped more people live longer than chemotherapy and transplant (4-year survival 54.6% vs 46.0%).
    Bispecific antibodiesGlofitamab, epcoritamab, mosunetuzumab
    What it is
    An "off-the-shelf" antibody with two arms: one grabs CD20 on the lymphoma cell, the other grabs CD3 on your own T cell, bringing them together so the T cell can kill the lymphoma cell. No cells need to be made, so treatment can start quickly.
    What it's like
    Given by drip or injection under the skin. The first doses are stepped up gradually to reduce CRS, which is usually mild and mostly happens in the first cycle. Some are given for a fixed time (for example 8 to 12 cycles); others continue longer.
    Evidence
    Used alone after two or more treatments, about 4 in 10 people with large B-cell lymphoma and 6 in 10 with follicular lymphoma had a complete response. In randomised trials, combinations improved survival in relapsed large B-cell lymphoma and progression-free survival in follicular lymphoma.
    Clinical trialsHow new treatments become standard

    Trials test new treatments carefully. Phase 1 trials find a safe dose, phase 2 trials check whether a treatment works, and phase 3 trials compare it with the current standard, usually by randomisation (a fair, chance-based allocation to each group). Many of today's standard treatments were available only in trials a few years ago.

    Ask your haematologist whether a trial suits you. You can also search Australian Cancer Trials, the ANZ Clinical Trials Registry and ClinicalTrials.gov.

    Complementary therapies and supplementsWhat the evidence says

    Exercise, cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT, a structured talking therapy that works on thoughts and habits) and mindfulness programs reduce cancer-related fatigue.

    American ginseng may help fatigue during treatment. Other dietary supplements tested did not improve fatigue.

    No supplement, diet or herbal product has been shown to treat lymphoma. Some can interact with cancer medicines, so always check with your team or pharmacist first.

    Treatment

    Side effects and when to get help

    Most side effects are expected, temporary and manageable. A few need help straight away. If you are unsure, call: your team would rather hear from you early.

    Get help now

    Call your 24-hour treatment line or go to emergency. Call 000 if life is in danger.

    • Temperature 38°C (100.4°F) or higher, shivering or shaking chills, or suddenly feeling unwell even without a fever
    • Trouble breathing, or chest pain
    • After CAR T-cell or bispecific treatment: fever, dizziness, confusion, unusual sleepiness, difficulty speaking or writing, shaking or a seizure
    • Bleeding that won't stop, black or bloody stools, or vomiting blood
    • New weakness or numbness in the legs, or losing control of bladder or bowel
    • Unable to keep fluids down

    Call your team today

    Same day, during or after hours.

    • Vomiting or diarrhoea not settling with your medicines
    • Mouth sores that stop you eating or drinking
    • A new rash
    • Pain, warmth or swelling in one leg or arm
    • Burning when passing urine, or a new cough
    • Numbness or tingling that is new or getting worse
    • Redness, swelling or fluid around your PICC line or port

    Mention at your next visit

    Write it down so it isn't forgotten.

    • Tiredness that limits your day
    • Mild tingling that isn't changing
    • Low mood, worry or poor sleep
    • Changes in memory or concentration
    • Sexual or relationship concerns
    • Money or work stress

    Common side effects and what helps

    FatigueThe most common symptom, during and after treatment

    Cancer-related fatigue is a heavy tiredness that rest doesn't fully fix. What helps most is regular activity matched to your energy, cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) and mindfulness programs. Tai chi or qigong can help during treatment, and yoga afterwards.

    Medicines such as antidepressants, wakefulness drugs or L-carnitine are not recommended for fatigue. Ask for blood tests if fatigue suddenly worsens, because anaemia (low red blood cells) or an underactive thyroid can add to it.

    Infection risk and low neutrophilsWhy a fever matters so much

    Neutrophils are the white blood cells that fight bacteria. Chemotherapy often lowers them for a week or two in each cycle, called neutropenia. A fever with neutropenia (febrile neutropenia) needs antibiotics quickly.

    • Wash hands often, and ask visitors with colds to stay away.
    • Practise normal food safety: wash produce, cook meat and eggs well, and avoid unpasteurised products. A strict "neutropenic diet" beyond food safety hasn't reduced infections in trials, which mostly included people with leukaemia.
    • Keep vaccines up to date, including for the people you live with.
    Numbness and tingling (peripheral neuropathy)From vincristine, brentuximab vedotin or polatuzumab vedotin

    Damage to the long nerves to the hands and feet causes tingling, numbness, burning or clumsiness. Tell your team early, because a dose change can stop it worsening. In most people it improves over months after treatment.

    Take care on stairs and uneven ground, check your feet daily if feeling is reduced, and use gloves when handling hot or cold items. Whether exercise prevents or treats neuropathy is not yet known.

    Nausea, mouth and bowelUsually well controlled with the right medicines

    Take anti-sickness medicines as prescribed, not only when you feel sick. Small, frequent meals and cool, bland foods are often easier. Use a soft toothbrush and a gentle salt-water mouth rinse. Vincristine and some anti-sickness medicines cause constipation, so ask about a bowel plan early.

    Hair loss and skinUsually temporary

    Hair loss with chemotherapy such as R-CHOP or ABVD usually starts 2 to 3 weeks into treatment and grows back after it ends. Skin in a radiotherapy area can become red or sore; use the creams your team recommends and protect it from the sun.

    CRS and ICANS after CAR T-cell or bispecific treatmentImmune reactions your team watches for closely

    Cytokine release syndrome (CRS) is a surge of immune signals that causes fever, low blood pressure or low oxygen. ICANS (immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome) affects the brain, causing confusion, trouble finding words or writing, drowsiness and, rarely, seizures. Teams grade both with a standard scale and treat them early.

    With bispecific antibodies, CRS is usually mild and mostly in the first cycle. With CAR T-cell therapy it is more common, and severe forms affect a minority.

    Thinking and memory ("chemo brain")Common, and usually improves

    Many people notice slower thinking, poorer memory or difficulty multitasking during and after treatment. Fatigue, poor sleep, low mood and some medicines add to it. Lists, phone reminders, one task at a time and rest breaks help. Tell your team if it affects work or safety.

    Living well

    Recovery and life after treatment

    Recovery is gradual. Energy, immunity and confidence usually rebuild over months, and knowing your own late-effect risks lets you stay ahead of them.

    What remission means

    Complete remission means tests show no active lymphoma. Partial remission means it has shrunk but some remains. For slow-growing types, the aim is often long-term control: many years of good health, with further treatment only if needed.

    Follow-up visits check how you feel, examine you and review blood tests. If you are well, routine scans usually aren't needed, because most relapses are found through symptoms between visits. Make sure you know how to get back in quickly if something worries you.

    Rebuilding immunity and vaccines

    Treatment, especially anti-CD20 antibodies, stem cell transplant and CAR T-cell therapy, can leave immunity low for months. Your vaccine history should be recorded at the start. Vaccines are re-given after transplant, CAR T-cell therapy or B-cell-depleting treatment, and the people you live with should be vaccinated too. Some live vaccines must be avoided for a time, so ask before any vaccine.

    Fertility, sex and relationships

    Some treatments, particularly alkylating chemotherapy (a group that includes cyclophosphamide, procarbazine and bendamustine), can reduce fertility or bring on early menopause. If you hope to have children, ask about fertility testing and options with a fertility specialist; methods exist both before and after treatment. Sexual changes are common and treatable, so they are worth raising.

    Your late-effects checklist

    Tick the treatments you have had. The list shows what is worth discussing with your GP and haematologist, with the strength of evidence for each. Many risks come from studies of people treated decades ago with older, more intensive methods, so they may overstate the risk of modern treatment.

    Work, study and money

    A gradual return, such as fewer hours or lighter duties at first, usually works better than going straight back to full time. A letter from your doctor can support changes at work. In Australia, Cancer Council (13 11 20) can connect you with financial, legal and workplace help. Elsewhere, national cancer charities offer similar help: see Support services.

    Living well

    Mind and emotions

    Lymphoma affects the whole person, not just the body. Fear, sadness, anger and numbness are normal responses to a frightening experience. They are not a sign of weakness, and there is good, proven help.

    You are far from alone

    38%

    of people seen in cancer and blood cancer services had depression, an anxiety disorder or an adjustment disorder, in studies that used clinical interviews.

    59%

    of people with or after cancer report at least moderate fear that it will come back, and 19% report high fear.

    4%

    met the criteria for post-traumatic stress disorder after a cancer diagnosis in clinical interviews, more than people without cancer. On questionnaires, 13% report trauma symptoms.

    An adjustment disorder is distress that is stronger or lasts longer than expected and makes daily life hard to manage. Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) means distressing memories, nightmares, avoidance and feeling constantly on guard that last more than a month. These figures come from people with many kinds of cancer, not only lymphoma.

    How feelings can change along the way

    There is no right way to feel, and feelings rarely move in a straight line. These are patterns that cancer psychology teams commonly see at each stage.

    1. Waiting and diagnosis

      Shock and uncertainty

      Numbness, disbelief, fear and trouble taking things in are common. Waiting for biopsy and scan results is often the hardest part.

    2. Watch and wait

      "Watch and worry"

      Hearing that a slow-growing lymphoma doesn't need treatment yet can bring relief and unease together. In a large trial, people who started rituximab adjusted to their illness and coped better over the first 7 months than people who were watched, so say if waiting feels hard.

    3. During treatment

      Tired, on hold, up and down

      Fatigue, mood swings and poor sleep on steroids, time away from work and friends, and staying home when infection risk is high can all wear you down.

    4. End of treatment

      "Now what?"

      Many people expect relief, then feel anxious or flat as appointments thin out and the team that kept watch steps back. This is common and usually eases with time and support.

    5. Life after treatment

      Living with uncertainty

      Fear of recurrence, anxiety before scans ("scanxiety") and a changed sense of self are common. Some people also notice positive changes, such as closer relationships or clearer priorities.

    6. If lymphoma comes back

      Facing it again

      Relapse can bring back earlier fears, along with tiredness from past treatment and hard decisions. Palliative care, offered alongside active treatment, can help with coping and quality of life.

    What are you feeling right now?

    Choose what fits best. You'll see what it can feel like, why it happens and what helps, with tools you can try straight away.

    If you are thinking about suicide or feel unsafe, please reach out now. In an emergency, call 000. The risk of suicide is higher after a cancer diagnosis than many people realise, especially in the first year, so these feelings deserve urgent and kind attention. Get help the same day, too, for sudden confusion or for seeing or hearing things others don't, which high-dose steroids occasionally cause.

    When to look for more help

    Many cancer services ask people to rate their distress from 0 (none) to 10 (extreme) on a "distress thermometer". A score of 4 or more is a signal to talk it through with your team, who can link you with the right support. Try the distress check-in.

    Guidelines recommend stepped care, which matches the help to how much distress you feel.

    • For everyone: clear information about what to expect, and a named person to ask.
    • For moderate symptoms: cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT, which works on the links between thoughts, feelings and actions); behavioural activation (gradually scheduling activities that matter to you); mindfulness-based programs; acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT, which builds the skill of making room for hard feelings while acting on your values); or structured physical activity.
    • For severe symptoms: psychological therapy, with medicines for some people, for example if therapy isn't accessible, isn't enough, or medicines have helped before.

    Talk to your team or GP (family doctor) soon if

    • you feel low or hopeless, or lose interest in things, most days for 2 weeks or more
    • worry or panic is hard to switch off, or stops you doing things
    • you can't sleep for weeks, or you sleep most of the day
    • you are drinking more, or using other substances, to cope
    • your mood swings on steroids feel extreme

    These are treatable. Your team can help directly or refer you to a psychologist, psychiatrist or psycho-oncology service.

    Getting psychological care

    Ask your treating team about psycho-oncology: psychological care for people affected by cancer, which many cancer centres offer. Social workers can help with practical worries such as money, work and housing.

    National cancer charities run free helplines staffed by nurses and counsellors, who talk with families as well as patients. Find services where you live.

    The whole picture: body, mind and life

    Recovery depends on how the body, the mind and the life around you interact. Small changes in one area often help the others.

    Body

    Energy and strength

    • Daily movement matched to energy
    • Regular sleep and wake times
    • Enough protein and fluids
    • Treat pain, nausea and constipation early
    Mind

    Thoughts and feelings

    • Name feelings rather than fight them
    • Worry time and paced breathing
    • CBT, ACT or mindfulness programs
    • Plan ahead for anniversaries and scan dates
    Life

    People, work and meaning

    • Tell people what kind of help you want
    • Peer support from others who have been through it
    • A gradual, planned return to work
    • Time for what matters most to you

        My plan

          Sleep reset

          Sleep problems are common during and after treatment. If poor sleep lasts more than a few weeks, ask about CBT for insomnia, which works well for people after cancer. These everyday steps draw on the same ideas.

          • Go to bed when sleepy, not just tired. If you're awake for about 20 minutes, get up and do something calm in dim light, then go back when sleepy.
          • Keep the bed for sleep and intimacy, not for scrolling or worrying.
          • Keep naps short (about 30 minutes) and before mid-afternoon.
          • Avoid caffeine from the afternoon, and alcohol close to bedtime.
          • Get daylight and some movement in the morning.
          • If you take steroids, ask whether you can take them in the morning.
          • Park worries in worry time earlier in the evening.

          Kind words

          Many people are far harder on themselves than they would ever be on a friend. A short self-compassion pause can soften that inner voice.

          Place a hand on your chest, or wherever feels comforting, and take one slow breath.

          Living well

          Family and carers

          Lymphoma happens to families, not just to one person. If you are caring for someone, your wellbeing matters too: looking after yourself is part of caring well.

          If you are caring for someone

          Feelings

          All of it is normal

          Worry, helplessness, exhaustion, guilt, frustration, grief and love can all arrive in the same day. Having hard feelings doesn't mean you are caring badly.

          Practical

          Be the second set of ears

          Come to appointments if they'd like you to, take notes, keep the team's 24-hour number handy, and learn the fever plan.

          Sharing the load

          Let others help

          Make a list of jobs people can take on, such as meals, lifts, school pick-ups and shopping, and say yes when they offer.

          Yourself

          Keep something for you

          Protect sleep, meals, movement and some time away. Keep your own health appointments, and talk to someone you trust.

          Programs that give carers information and teach coping skills reduce the strain of caring and improve carers' coping and quality of life.

          Supporting without taking over

          • Ask what kind of help they want today: practical help, company, distraction or someone to listen.
          • Let them lead on how much they want to talk about the illness. It's fine to talk about other things.
          • Listen more than you fix. "That sounds really hard" often helps more than advice.
          • Nobody needs to stay positive all the time. Making room for fear and sadness helps people cope.
          • Agree together who will share updates with family and friends, so the person with lymphoma doesn't have to repeat them.

          These suggestions reflect common advice from cancer support services.

          Signs you need support too

          • You feel exhausted most of the time, or can't sleep
          • You feel low, anxious or irritable most days
          • You've stopped doing anything for yourself
          • You are drinking more, or feel you can't cope

          Talk to your own GP (family doctor), and contact carer support services.

          Talking with children and teenagers

          Children usually sense when something is wrong. Simple, honest information in words they understand tends to worry them less than silence.

          Under 5

          Keep it simple and steady

          Use short, simple words, keep routines as normal as you can, and reassure them often that they are loved and will be looked after.

          5 to 12

          Name it and explain changes

          Use the word "lymphoma" and explain what will change day to day. Tell them they can't catch it, and that nothing they did or thought caused it.

          Teenagers

          Include and respect

          Give more detail and include them in plans. Respect their need for friends and independence, and watch for withdrawal or anger.

          All ages

          Keep the door open

          Let them ask anything, tell them when things change, and let their school know so teachers can support them.

          Couples, intimacy and sex

          Lymphoma and its treatment can change energy, body image, desire and roles within a relationship. Sexual problems are common and treatable, so they are worth raising with your team, together or separately. Counselling for individuals or couples, and practical treatments such as lubricants, vaginal moisturisers or erection medicines, can help.

          During treatment, ask your team about contraception and any precautions with sex, because some treatments can harm a pregnancy.

          Young adults

          Hodgkin lymphoma is most common in teenagers and young adults. Fertility, study, work, money, dating and friendships can all be affected. Ask early about fertility preservation, ideally before treatment starts. Youth cancer services and youth cancer charities offer support designed for this age group. Find services where you live.

          When someone is very unwell

          Palliative care is specialist care for symptoms, stress and planning. It can start at any stage, alongside treatment aimed at control or cure, and it supports families as well. If the person you care for dies, bereavement support is available through palliative care services and cancer charities.

          Carers can call the same helplines as patients. See all services where you live.

          Living well

          Moving and eating well

          Exercise is one of the best-supported ways to feel better during and after lymphoma treatment. Food matters too, mainly for keeping strength up, not for treating the lymphoma.

          Why moving helps

          In a Canadian trial of 122 people with lymphoma, 12 weeks of supervised aerobic exercise improved physical functioning, quality of life, fatigue, depression and fitness, without affecting how many people completed chemotherapy or how well it worked.

          Across 18 trials in people with blood cancers, exercise probably reduced fatigue and may ease depression; effects on quality of life were unclear. Across all cancers, the evidence that exercise improves anxiety, depression, fatigue, physical function and quality of life is strong.

          Exercising safely during treatment

          • Fever or infection: rest, and call your team.
          • Low platelets: avoid contact sports and activities with a high risk of falls or knocks.
          • Low neutrophils: prefer home or outdoor exercise to crowded gyms.
          • PICC line or port: avoid swimming until the line is out, and protect the dressing.
          • Numb feet or poor balance: choose a stationary bike or supported exercise, and keep a hand near support.
          • After anthracyclines or chest radiotherapy: build up gradually, and report chest pain, palpitations or unusual breathlessness.
          • Anaemia: use effort and breathing as your guide rather than pace or distance.

          These are consensus precautions from clinical practice rather than trial findings. An accredited exercise physiologist or physiotherapist experienced in cancer care can tailor a program.

          For exercise professionals and physiotherapistsPrescription details and screening

          The 2019 roundtable found exercise testing and training generally safe, and advised that no fitness assessment is needed before starting low-intensity walking, cycling or resistance work. It refers to NCCN survivorship guidance for when medical clearance or supervision is needed, based on treatment effects. Evidence for exercise as a treatment for chemotherapy-induced neuropathy and cognitive change is insufficient.

          Practical considerations for lymphoma: anthracycline and mediastinal radiotherapy exposure (cardiac risk, so progress gradually with symptom monitoring); neuropathy (balance and falls); steroid exposure (bone health, so include progressive resistance and impact as tolerated); thrombocytopenia (avoid contact and high fall risk); central lines (avoid swimming and loading that pulls on the line); post-CAR T-cell or post-transplant cytopenias (infection control).

          Tools and sources

          What's new, 2024 to 2026

          The main research results and approvals of the last few years. Approvals listed are US decisions; availability and funding differ by country, including in Australia, so ask your haematologist what is available to you.

          1. Apr–Jun 2026

            Sonrotoclax for relapsed mantle cell lymphoma

            A second-generation BCL2 inhibitor received accelerated US approval after two or more treatments including a BTK inhibitor. Accelerated approval rests on early results; confirmatory trials continue.

          2. Mar 2026

            Nivolumab-AVD approved for advanced Hodgkin lymphoma

            The US approved nivolumab-AVD for previously untreated stage III–IV classic Hodgkin lymphoma from age 12. At 3 years in S1826, 91% were progression-free versus 82% with brentuximab vedotin-AVD (conference abstract, December 2025).

          3. Early 2026

            All-tablet, fixed-length treatment for CLL and SLL

            Acalabrutinib with venetoclax for 14 months was approved in the US for untreated CLL and small lymphocytic lymphoma, after the AMPLIFY trial showed longer progression-free survival than chemo-immunotherapy.

          4. Jan 2026

            Breast checks after anthracyclines

            International guidance now suggests breast surveillance for women treated young with 200 mg/m² or more of doxorubicin, even without chest radiotherapy.

          5. Dec 2025

            Two advances for relapsed follicular lymphoma

            Adding epcoritamab (EPCORE FL-1) or tafasitamab (inMIND) to lenalidomide-rituximab lengthened progression-free survival in phase 3 trials. The US approved the epcoritamab combination (the tafasitamab combination had been approved in June 2025) and liso-cel CAR T-cell therapy for relapsed marginal zone lymphoma.

          6. Jun 2025

            Over-60s with Hodgkin lymphoma; easier CAR T-cell rules

            In people 60 and over in S1826, 2-year progression-free survival was 89% with nivolumab-AVD versus 64% with brentuximab vedotin-AVD. Separately, the US cut the required stay near the centre and the no-driving period after CAR T-cell therapy to 2 weeks.

          7. May 2025

            Acalabrutinib for older people with mantle cell lymphoma

            In ECHO, adding acalabrutinib to bendamustine-rituximab lengthened progression-free survival in people 65 and over (median 66.4 vs 49.6 months), without a difference in overall survival so far.

          8. Nov 2024

            Glofitamab-GemOx for relapsed large B-cell lymphoma

            In STARGLO, people who could not have a transplant lived longer with glofitamab plus GemOx chemotherapy than with rituximab-GemOx (median 25.5 vs 12.9 months).

          9. Oct 2024

            S1826 published

            Nivolumab-AVD outperformed brentuximab vedotin-AVD in advanced Hodgkin lymphoma (2-year progression-free survival 92% vs 83%) and was better tolerated.

          10. Jul 2024

            BrECADD for advanced Hodgkin lymphoma

            In HD21, BrECADD was gentler and more effective than escalated BEACOPP (4-year progression-free survival 94.3% vs 90.9%).

          11. May 2024

            Ibrutinib moves into first treatment for younger people with mantle cell lymphoma

            In TRIANGLE, adding ibrutinib raised 3-year failure-free survival to 88% from 72%; whether transplant still adds benefit is being studied. ASCO also updated its guidance on cancer-related fatigue.

          12. Mar 2024

            Vaccination guidance for adults with cancer

            ASCO's guideline on vaccinating adults with cancer covers timing, re-vaccination after transplant and CAR T-cell therapy, and vaccinating household contacts.

            How evidence is graded here

            Strong

            Consistent results from randomised trials (studies that compare treatments by chance allocation) or very large, well-conducted studies in the relevant group. Unlikely to change.

            Moderate

            Good evidence with limits: single-arm studies, subgroup results, guideline consensus on reviewed evidence, or results extrapolated from other cancers. Could change.

            Weak

            Small, early or indirect evidence, or clinical consensus without trials. A reasonable approach, but uncertain.

            Not supported

            Tested and not shown to help, evidence points against it, or never tested despite being promoted.

            It is a plain-language summary and is not the NHMRC or GRADE system. Statistics, care standards and regulatory decisions carry no grade; they are labelled by source.
